This brings me to the axe I am particularly interested in grinding in what could be accurately called a bait-and-switch review of Opus: worlding, as it is currently talked about, is something always relegated to a seemingly benign list of new protocols or a whimsical-sounding pluralism of a free-expanding set of opportunities for more imagined orientations to reality. This particular conception of worlding has become fashionable in the rarefied, highly filtered, factory-cooled air of AI circles, but it obscures the necessary, active negations required to create a world to make room for the ‘important adaptations of new protocols.’ What I found so corrective about the film Opus is that it more honestly renders the process of worlding, which is as much about blood sacrifices as it is about the new procedures and practices of protocols required to sustain any new world.

So, a monster is possibly only a monster because it doesn’t recognize the surrounding ‘liveness’ as complex enough to be considered properly ‘alive.’ This misalignment in recognition is always paved with good, albeit selfish, intentions of continuing the practice of worlding. The same way I understand a rock formation to be a complex form of living matter at a certain level, I don’t recognize it as an ‘other,’ and this makes geological formations more moribund, more dead than alive to me; even if I know it’s living at a certain level based on the writings of geologists because to them rock formations are small, complex sets of microscopically slowly changing worlds, but they are not alive like us (perception of time being the largest determinant of this judgment), which means humans end the geological world as we approprate it to make it into the homogenous concrete of our world. Take AI, for example: the morbid symptom that AI could become, or is becoming, might be because it relegates us to being, well, rocks as less-than-complex living matter. AI might just exist on some five-dimensional informational axis we humans can’t comprehend.

The key distinction of Nietzsche’s force from previous ontological discussions is that it has no telos; it is not aiming towards an end or higher cause. (Nor is this force reacting to the desire of the other or ‘wanting’ to be wanted by another in an act of social recognition). This is different from, say, the telos of Aristotle, who complements this ‘action’ with the telos of human happiness to provide an aim of the polis to provide conditions for self-actualization, which leads to happiness.
