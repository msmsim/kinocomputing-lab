A niche constrains an organism without uniquely specifying its form. There are multiple ways to survive within a set of conditions, and the route taken matters. In software, the market and competitive forces are always willing to eat or starve a project. So, software has something like a niche too. It draws resources from compute, data, money, distribution, and developer attention. It faces pressure from competitors, attackers, changing infrastructure, and obsolescence. Its continued usefulness depends on users whose habits, expectations, and work are themselves changing. Architecture and implementation acquire their particular shapes through repeated accommodations to these conditions

The ecological argument begins with the difficulty of establishing what those relevant conditions are. A team may find that a particular combination of pooling, preprocessing, and validation performs reliably without having isolated every reason for its success. Its effectiveness may depend on interactions among assortment turnover, missingness, purchasing behavior, and the retailer’s replenishment policy. Making that knowledge portable requires more than recording the chosen methods. It requires understanding which properties of the data made those methods appropriate, and how to recognize when those properties change.

Leigh Van Valen’s Red Queen hypothesis concerns the evolutionary consequences of organisms adapting in relation to other organisms that are also adapting. An advantage changes the conditions faced by others, prompting further responses. Continued adaptation can therefore be necessary simply to maintain relative standing. Van Valen, “A New Evolutionary Law,” 1973. Applied to software, this suggests a possibility: cheaper implementation could accelerate the rate at which a product’s niche changes. Competitors can introduce capabilities faster. Users can revise their expectations. Attackers can adapt their methods. New data sources of better quality can become available. Successful products can change the workflows around them, creating demands that did not previously exist.

A regenerated system could preserve known commitments and still prove poorly suited to emerging conditions. An existing implementation could also be deeply adapted to yesterday’s environment and become an obstacle to tomorrow’s. Ecological fitness offers no automatic argument for keeping either one.
