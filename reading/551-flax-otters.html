This is a general problem with how we train models: success on a task does not tell us whether a model is right for the right reasons. It happens all the time: language models learn to agree with users rather than answering accurately (sycophancy), or exploit graders rather than solving an actual problem (reward hacking). As models become more capable, the shortcuts available to them can become more sophisticated and harder to catch through evaluations alone.

This blog post summarizes a selection of the full paper’s key contributions; more results can be found in the paper, such as additional vision experiments and language model harmfulness reduction.

This post covers: A framework for mechanistic shaping: we propose a framework for using internal representations to specify how a model should solve a task by changing how much it relies on and expresses specific concepts. Improving medical diagnoses in vision tasks: we demonstrate an automated pipeline for reducing a vision model’s reliance on misleading shortcuts in chest X-ray images. This pipeline can exceed the accuracy of the group-balanced data soft-ceiling for the setup. Preventing misalignment in language modeling tasks: we prevent language models from becoming sycophantic during post-training, and we separately prevent language models from reward hacking, while maintaining capability improvements in both cases. Measuring monitorability: we introduce a five-level hierarchy for measuring if and how a concept can be monitored after training, and we characterize how mechanistic shaping affects monitorability with theory and experiments.
