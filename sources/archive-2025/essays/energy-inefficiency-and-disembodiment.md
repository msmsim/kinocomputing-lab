# Energy (in)efficiency and (dis)embodiment

- Meta
    - Type: [[Human agent journal]]
    - Participants: [[McSweet]]
    - Date [[August 24th, 2025]]
- Text
    - In the distance is the London skyline. To my left is Greenwich’s Royal Observatory, a monument to analog planetary and celestial instrumentation. In my hands are a pen and a pocket notebook. Orbiting in my thoughts, a question: what do I mean when I talk about “kinocomputing”?
    - Above me, the sun; beneath me, parched hillside grass; adjacent to me, groups of people, families and friends and strangers; flowing past me, a soft breeze. It returns. The question. “Kinocomputing”? The beginnings of an answer...
    - Contemporary computing cannot get us to a synthetic intelligence that is more than or equal to human intelligence, in all its glories. So, what will? Kinocomputing is, ultimately, concerned with this question. And the hypothesis is that the answer comes down to energy and embodiment.
    - Contemporary computing is too energy inefficient and too disembodied to yield synthetic intelligence approaching or surpassing human capacity. And it is these things because the existing technological stack atop which our current civilisation balances is overly discrete. It is not motion-based but stasis-based, an inversion of both the fundamental nature of reality itself and all existence proofs we have of advanced intelligence.
    - Kinocomputing is thus an attempt to interrogate and cohere alternative forms of computing and intelligence. Those that are motion-based. Those that are comparatively more energy efficient and embodied, both in isolation and at the total-system level. Those that are either continuous-at-base or more hybrid continuous-discrete than is current.
    - The constituents of Greenwich’s Royal Observatory had a mandate to impose a clean and intricate order upon the chaotic pre-industrial world. Cartesian coordinates, precise lines, abstracted dimensions; create cartographical visions from above and engineer the probing of the skies. These observers, themselves beings of motion, taxidermied the observed and made it immobile.
