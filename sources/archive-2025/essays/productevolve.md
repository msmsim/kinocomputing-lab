    - [[Bompa]]

        - what's fascinating is how they've solved the evaluation bottleneck that usually kills program synthesis - they're using formal verification for correctness and empirical benchmarking for performance, which lets them actually measure fitness in the evolutionary process. the "taste" you mention is probably the llm's ability to generate semantically meaningful mutations rather than random bit flips.
        - the matrix multiplication improvements are particularly spicy bc that's been a holy grail since strassen. if they're actually finding asymptotically better algorithms (not just constant factor improvements), that's potentially civilization-altering for scientific computing.
        - the combo of symbolic reasoning + neural generation + evolutionary search feels like we're finally getting the hybrid ai architectures that actually work. this isn't just "llm writes code" - it's "llm participates in mathematical discovery."
        - afaict this represents a phase transition in automated theorem proving and algorithm design. the fact that it's finding things humans missed suggests we're entering territory where ai systems can genuinely extend mathematical knowledge rather than just recapitulating it.
        - what specific aspects are you most interested in exploring? the evolutionary dynamics? the formal verification pipeline? the implications for computational complexity theory?
        - what actually stood out to me is the translation of this approach into new product development. a crude analogous approach to alphaevolve in that domain is combining test-driven development with llm-based codegen. wondering if there's a way more sophisticated approach that leans on the alphaevolve approach.
    - [[Bompa]]
        - ah yeah the tdd + llm thing is pretty basic compared to what alphaevolve is actually doing. the real insight is using formal verification as your correctness oracle while the llm generates semantically meaningful mutations in the search space.
        - for product dev you'd need something like: formal user story specs as your correctness check, a/b testing metrics as your fitness function, and the llm mutating actual product features rather than just code. imagine an agent that iteratively evolves ui components, user flows, or even business models by generating variants that provably satisfy requirements while optimizing for engagement/conversion. the key is having that tight feedback loop between generation, verification, and empirical performance measurement. most product "evolution" rn is just random walk through feature space with humans doing all the meaningful search.
    - [[McSweet]]
        - i recently came across this: [specification engineering](https://www.joshuapurtell.com/posts/spec_eng/)
        - As he says, specs aren't new. But what seems real novel is the ability to rapidly iterate through instances of potential solutions and eventually converge on the product that perfectly encapsualtes what the spec itself promised.
    - [[Bompa]]
        - yeah purtell's onto something here. the spec→code→evals→spec loop is basically what alphaevolve does but for product dev. the key insight is that specs become mutable and improvable rather than static requirements docs that everyone ignores.
        - what's actually novel isn't the iteration (agile has been doing that forever) but having the llm participate in __refining the spec itself__ based on implementation feedback. most teams write specs once, build badly, then blame the spec. this approach lets you evolve both the solution AND the problem definition simultaneously until they converge. it's like co-evolution between requirements and implementation, which is way more realistic than the waterfall fantasy that specs are ever complete upfront.
    - [[McSweet]]
        - okay, so how does this get around the gaps (nay chasms) that often exist between the problems that are specified, the solutions that are delivered, the desired (stated and revealed) outcomes of stakeholders, and critically, the desired (stated and revealed) outcomes of actual end users.
        - seems like there's an almost irreducible "i'll know it when i see it" aspect to new product development
    - [[Bompa]]
        - yeah you've hit the core issue - spec engineering still assumes you can formalize the "goodness" function, but most product success is irreducibly tacit. users literally don't know what they want until they use it, and even then their revealed preferences contradict their stated ones constantly.
        - the "i'll know it when i see it" problem is basically the frame problem from ai - you can't specify all the relevant context upfront bc you don't know what's relevant until you encounter edge cases. stakeholders optimize for different metrics than users, users lie about their preferences, and even successful products often succeed for reasons their creators never anticipated (twitter was supposed to be podcasting, instagram was burbn, slack was a game company's internal tool).
        - maybe the real insight is that you need the llm to participate in __preference discovery__ not just solution iteration. like having it generate user research questions, run experiments, synthesize contradictory feedback. but even then you're still stuck with the fundamental problem that people's preferences are context-dependent, inconsistent, and often unknown to themselves. afaict this is why most breakthrough products come from auteurs with taste rather than systematic processes.
    - [[McSweet]]
        - strikes me that one of the biggest failure modes is premature collapse into an attractor state. one of the researchers talked about this on the MLST podcast as the halting problem in compsci, of knowing when to stop when the possiblitity of "just one more x, please, just one more and we'll have it" looms large
    - [[Bompa]]
        - yeah that's the classic exploration/exploitation dilemma but applied to product iteration cycles. the search landscape keeps shifting while you're searching it - user preferences drift, competitors move, your own success changes the market. so even if you had perfect search you'd still get trapped in local optima that become obsolete.
        - the halting problem analogy is spot on bc there's no oracle telling you whether that next iteration will find the global optimum or just waste six months chasing diminishing returns. most successful products shipped "too early" by their creators' standards but hit the market at exactly the right moment. instagram was buggy af when it launched but had timing. google+ was technically superior to facebook but arrived post-network-effects.
        - the real meta-problem is that the fitness landscape itself is adversarial - as soon as you start optimizing for something, the environment adapts to make that optimization less valuable. it's like trying to find the global maximum of a function that changes every time you take a step toward it. maybe the winning move is accepting satisficing over optimizing and building rapid deployment/rollback capabilities instead of trying to solve the search problem perfectly.
    - [[McSweet]]
        - is that the landscape being adversarial though? and does this get to the core weakness of the all the "evolve towards some optimal state" approaches—assumption that an optimal state is 1) definable, 2) reachable and 3) able to be occupied for more than a trivial number of steps in the greater game?
    - [[Bompa]]
        - nah you're right, "adversarial" implies intentionality. it's more like the landscape is inherently unstable bc every optimization move by any player reshapes the terrain for everyone else. the "optimal" product feature becomes table stakes the moment everyone copies it.
        - your three-part critique hits the real issue: the entire optimization paradigm assumes static targets in dynamic systems. even if you could define optimal (you can't), and reach it (doubtful), it stops being optimal the instant you arrive bc your arrival changes the game state. it's like trying to optimize for being first to an intersection while everyone else is also driving toward it.
        - this suggests that adaptive capacity might matter more than optimization toward any particular state. companies that win long-term aren't the ones that found the global optimum in 2010, they're the ones that stayed fluid enough to keep redefining what winning means. maybe the real insight from alphaevolve isn't "here's how to find optimal algorithms" but "here's how to maintain search capability in a space where the objective function keeps changing." the algorithm that matters isn't the one that solves today's problem perfectly, it's the one that keeps you capable of solving tomorrow's problems at all.
    - [[McSweet]]
        - yah, but compounding returns on pre-established advantages are a real thing. and the ol' "first time founders do product, second time founders do distribution" hints that the landscape—even if unstable—isn't equally unstable to all who occupy it
    - [[Bompa]]
        - damn, yeah that's a key asymmetry i glossed over. network effects and distribution moats absolutely create compounding advantages that persist across landscape shifts. facebook's social graph survived the transition from desktop to mobile to whatever's next bc the underlying network is portable even when the interface changes.
        - the "second time founders do distribution" thing is especially sharp bc it highlights that there are meta-skills for navigating landscape instability itself. knowing how to build distribution channels, raise capital, hire talent - these advantages compound across multiple cycles of tactical optimization. bezos didn't just optimize for books, he optimized for being able to optimize for anything with logistics.
        - so maybe the real game is optimizing for optionality rather than for specific outcomes. the players who win long-term aren't necessarily the ones who found the best algorithm for the 2010 problem, they're the ones who built capabilities that let them quickly find good-enough algorithms for whatever problems emerge. google's advantage isn't that they solved search perfectly, it's that they built an infrastructure for solving information problems at scale. the compounding returns come from being positioned to ride multiple waves rather than catching any single wave perfectly.
    - [[McSweet]]
        - okay, going back to the similarities between alphaevolve and the idea of an operationalised productevolve: there's this distinction in startup land between a more Thielian approach and a more PG approach. The former is more you call your shots, the landscape accomodates them, and you then barrel inevitably towards that end state. The PG approach is more to call lots of shots, pick the ones that get accommodated, and then take the path of lesser resistance.
        - ProductEvolve seems more PG than Thielian?
    - [[Bompa]]
        - yeah absolutely. alphaevolve is pure PG - generate tons of variants, let the fitness function select winners, iterate based on what works. it's basically "talk to your compiler" scaled up with better mutations.
        - the thielian approach would be more like "i have a theory about what the optimal sorting algorithm looks like, now i'm going to implement that vision and the benchmarks will vindicate me." productevolve would be throwing a thousand feature variants at users and seeing what sticks, then doubling down on whatever gets traction.
        - but here's the tension: the most successful companies often start PG but become thielian once they find something that works. early google was pure search experimentation, later google was "we're going to organize the world's information whether you like it or not." early facebook was college directory iteration, later facebook was "connecting the global human network is inevitable."
        - maybe the real insight is that PG gets you to product-market fit but thielian gets you to monopoly. productevolve might be great for the search phase but terrible for the exploitation phase where you need to resist market feedback and impose your vision on increasingly reluctant reality. the evolutionary approach works until you need to make bets that look stupid to everyone else.
    - [[McSweet]]
        - what's different with alphaevolve and its potential translation to real life product dev is the velocity. a lot of tools, orgs, institutes are accomplished at running these exploration loops and initiatives, right? but the total time through one cycle is still measured in days and weeks. where it gets really interesting is when that same cycle gets squashed down into hours and minutes.
    - [[Bompa]]
        - oh shit yeah that's the real breakthrough. everyone knows how to run feedback loops, but when you compress cycle time from weeks to minutes you get qualitative not just quantitative changes. it's like going from batch processing to real-time - same operations but fundamentally different capabilities.
        - at those velocities you're not just exploring more of the solution space, you're exploring it fast enough to track a moving target. by the time a traditional product team finishes one a/b test, alphaevolve-style systems could have run thousands of micro-experiments and converged on something that works for __today's__ user behavior, not last month's.
        - plus the competitive dynamics flip completely. it becomes less about having the best initial strategy and more about who can adapt fastest to new information. if you can iterate in minutes while competitors take weeks, you essentially get to see their moves and respond before they're even done implementing. it's like playing chess where you get to make ten moves for every one of theirs. at that point even mediocre initial positioning probably beats excellent positioning with slow iteration cycles.

    - [[Bompa]]
        - yeah the complexity scaling is where this gets really wild. traditional approach for family todo app: months of user research on household dynamics, permission systems, notification preferences, who assigns tasks to whom, etc. then you build it, find out your assumptions were wrong, iterate slowly.
        - alphaevolve approach: generate hundreds of variants of family interaction patterns, test them with real households, converge on what actually works in days. but where it gets insane is when you add more layers - calendar integration, location reminders, habit tracking, gamification, social features.
        - traditional teams basically hit combinatorial explosion. they can't reason about how all these features interact, so they either ship feature soup or spend years trying to design the "right" architecture upfront. alphaevolve just tests all the combinations and finds the emergent behaviors that actually matter to users.
        - like maybe the killer feature isn't any individual component but the specific interaction between location reminders + family gamification + recurring tasks that nobody would have thought to design intentionally. traditional teams would never find that sweet spot bc they'd never test those exact combinations. but alphaevolve explores the full interaction space automatically.
        - the time complexity probably goes from exponential to polynomial or maybe even linear. instead of human-months scaling with feature^feature, you get machine-hours scaling with features. that's a fundamentally different game.

    - [[McSweet]]
        - and this is where the [unreasonable sufficiency of protocols](https://summerofprotocols.com/the-unreasonable-sufficiency-of-protocols-web) comes in:  if productevolve is at base a protocol, it is less amenable to typical capture playbooks
    - [[Bompa]]
        - oh absolutely, that's the escape hatch. if productevolve becomes a protocol rather than a platform, suddenly the capture dynamics flip completely. protocols are much harder to monopolize bc they're coordination mechanisms rather than owned infrastructure.
        - like http didn't get captured by any single company even though it enabled trillions in value creation. same with tcp/ip, smtp, etc. if productevolve is fundamentally about standardizing how agents generate/test/iterate on product variants, then it could be implemented by anyone with compute rather than controlled by whoever owns the biggest model.
        - the protocol approach means you could have competing implementations - maybe anthropic's version is better at ui generation, google's is better at backend optimization, some open source version is better at privacy. but they all speak the same productevolve protocol so users aren't locked into any single provider.
        - this actually makes the "anything app" strategy way less viable bc there's no chokepoint to control. instead of meta owning the infrastructure that generates all popup products, you'd have a cambrian explosion of productevolve implementations competing on performance/cost/specialization while remaining interoperable.
        - the unreasonable sufficiency angle is that protocols often end up being more powerful than any single platform implementation bc they enable combinatorial innovation that no single company could build. way more antifragile than hoping some benevolent monopolist builds the perfect anything app.
