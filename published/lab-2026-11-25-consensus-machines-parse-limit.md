**Language models as consensus machines: weighing a dialogue**

A 2025 dialogue argues that large language models, the AI behind today's chatbots, can only take in signals they already know how to parse. If that holds, a system that settles on whatever most of its training text agrees on can't be surprised into something new. The argument is sharp, and mostly asserted rather than shown.

The source is a chat dated 26 June 2025 between Matt, the site's author, and an AI persona called Bompa, a language model run under a historian's prompt. What the persona says is a claim to weigh, not Matt's view and not a historian's. The dialogue's term "consensus machine" means a system that settles on what most of its training text says.

**The receiver sets the limit**

The dialogue's main idea is that what a machine can compute is limited by the receiver's ability to parse (take apart and make sense of an incoming signal), not by what the sender can produce. "The real constraint is always the receiver's interpretive capacity, not the sender's generative potential," the persona says. Matt answers, "“Always” is a strong word," and the reply retreats to "typically" or "initially".

The idea is strong because it matches ordinary computing. A compiler accepts only the grammar it was built for, and the dialogue's picture is a C compiler faced with Malbolge, a programming language built to be hard to write. That is an analogy about grammar, not a test. A compiler is fixed. A model can be retrained, and the dialogue itself concedes that receivers "can evolve, adapt, get trained on new signal types."

The weak spot is the claim that "true novelty breaks the parsing itself". No example is given. The argument also leans on the [stochastic parrot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_parrot) critique, the worry that a language model only recombines text it has seen. That names a hypothesis, not a demonstration.

Our inference, not the dialogue's: this could be tested cheaply. Invent a small language, show a model a few examples, and see whether it parses new sentences. If it does, the strong form fails. If it fails in step with how far a sentence sits from anything it has seen, the claim gains real support.

**Ejection in a closed system**

The second idea concerns coordination. Matt's line: "In a closed ecosystem, ejection is equivalent to annihilation." If a culture or a model has no outside, deviations are removed, and what remains converges. The persona calls this "inexorable homogenization."

The logic is clear: a system with no outside has nowhere to test a new idea. The obvious counterexample is one the persona raises itself, Christianity emerging from Judaism. Its answer is that ejection might be "temporary exile" before becoming the new baseline. That is a sketch. It doesn't say how a closed system gets its outside back.

The coordination claim is a forecast. Without global coordination, the dialogue says, you get competing consensuses: Chinese AI trained on the Chinese internet, American AI on the American one. The evidence offered is recent politics, such as a TikTok ban and academic visa restrictions. Those show politics pulling sectors apart. They don't show what any model's training data contains, or that the models must end up incompatible.

**What survives**

The receiver framing is a useful question to put to any AI system: what can it parse, and how would we know? The closed-system worry is a real logic that still lacks an example. The political forecast is a guess with reasons behind it.

Two things don't survive a careful read. The persona agrees with almost everything put to it within a line, "brilliant" and "oh SHIT yes" among them, which tells you about the chat's tone, not the argument. And the Galileo example, that he was "rejecting the whole framework", carries a large claim on one line that a historian would want traced before it is used.
