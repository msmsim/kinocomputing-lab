**Optical neural networks: the 1,000x figure is for the light**

An influential 2017 paper on optical neural networks promises energy efficiency "three orders of magnitude" better than electronics. We read the full paper. The number follows from the paper's own arithmetic, but that arithmetic is dominated by, and as far as we can tell limited to, the optical power needed to make the light behave nonlinearly. The chip in the paper's own experiment needed far more than the estimate just to hold its settings. We rate the claim *overstretched*: true as a bound on one cost, wrong as a prediction of what such a system would use.

**The story as told.** [Shen and colleagues (arXiv:1610.02365, Nature Photonics 2017)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1610.02365) built a programmable silicon-photonic chip, an array of 56 interferometers (devices that split and recombine light to do a matrix multiplication), and ran a small neural network on it for recognising four spoken vowels. Its abstract promises a speed gain of at least two orders of magnitude and three orders of magnitude in power efficiency.

**What the paper says.** Three separate things.

- *The experiment.* A network of 4 layers of 4 neurons, 48 trained phase settings. On the 180-case test set it got 138 right (76.7%); the same network simulated on a computer got 165 (91.7%). The 76.7% is the best-calibrated of three runs; the paper says the other two, less well calibrated, "exceeded 70%". That is a real demonstration of optical matrix multiplication, with a visible accuracy gap. The paper says its demonstration implements the nonlinear step "in the electronic domain": the light was detected, the nonlinearity applied electronically, and the result injected into the next layer.
- *The speed claim.* "At least two orders of magnitude faster" assumes a 100 GHz photodetection rate. We found no sign in the text that the experiment ran at that rate.
- *The energy claim.* The paper estimates that power is set by the light needed to trigger an optical nonlinearity (a saturable absorber, assumed to switch at 1 megawatt per square centimetre) with enough signal-to-noise: about N milliwatts for N neurons per layer. With its own speed assumption that gives 5/(mN) femtojoules per operation for m layers, so 0.3 femtojoules for the demonstrated network (we recomputed this; the paper's printed formula lost its fraction bar, and only the 5/(mN) reading reproduces its own "tens of millions of times" at a very large network) and far less at scale, against about 1 picojoule per operation for CPUs and GPUs "not including the power spent on data movement". A femtojoule is a thousandth of a picojoule.

**Where it stretches.** The paper says power is "dominated by" the nonlinearity's optical power. Our inference, since we did not read the supplement with the full calculation, is that the estimate leaves out the laser, modulators, detectors and converters. The paper does mention the energy to hold the phase settings: its chip needs about 10 milliwatts per phase modulator to maintain its settings, and suggests phase-change materials that need no power as a future fix. At that average, holding the 48 trained settings costs roughly 0.5 watts, at least 100 times the paper's 4 milliwatt estimate for the whole 4-neuron system. That is a lower bound (the chip has 213 phase shifters), and it compares the demonstration chip with a projected system that the paper says would use non-volatile phase-change materials; the paper states that caveat itself. Later hardware shows a gap of the same kind, though it is a different chip and architecture, so this is illustration, not confirmation: a 2026 optical processor reported [about 20 attojoules per multiply-accumulate in the light and 250 femtojoules for the whole system](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-68452-x), a ratio over 10,000. And the comparison in the 2017 paper is not like for like: its electronic figure is a processor-level number (taken from its reference 40, and "not including the power spent on data movement"), while the optical one is nonlinearity-only.

**Verdict.** Overstretched. The paper's text is largely careful: it says "in principle", it names the heater power and it says its nonlinearity was electronic. The abstract's promise and the "tens of millions of times" line are the stretch, if read as a system's energy. What survives: optical matrix multiplication works on chip, at small scale and with reduced accuracy, and the light itself can be very cheap. What doesn't: any claim about total energy per operation.

**How sure we are.** Checked against the full paper text. We did not read its supplement, which holds the detailed power calculation, and we did not survey how other papers cite it, so "overstretched" is about the paper's headline, not about any particular citer.
