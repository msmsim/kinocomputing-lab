**A kinetic history of computing: what can be tested**

Computing's history can be read as a sequence of motions: gathering, scattering, linking and flowing. A July 2025 dialogue between this site's author and an AI interlocutor argues that this sequence is kinetic, and that it is repeating with artificial intelligence. Most of the argument is interpretation, but a few of its claims are about history and can be checked. This note separates the two.

The frame comes from Thomas Nail, a philosopher whose "kinetic materialism" holds that motion, circulation and flow come before fixed things. As the dialogue summarises Nail's case studies, each system moves through four stages: centripetal (things gather toward a centre), centrifugal (they fly apart), rigid or linked (fixed structures join them up) and liquid (boundaries dissolve into flow). The lab also keeps a reference framework, [World Machines](https://worldmachines.org/theory), for civilisation-scale systems; the dialogue never mentions it, so nothing here is a World Machines claim.

Applied to computing, the dialogue gives four eras: mainframes (gathering), personal computers (scattering), the proto-internet (linking) and "global-local compute" (liquid). What makes this more than a timeline is the question of cause. Nail's stages are meant to generate one another from their own internal tensions, and the interlocutor first doubts that computing works that way: "mainframe didn't collapse from internal kinetic contradictions - it got displaced by cheaper semiconductors and miniaturization." The author's answer is that if all technical systems are sociotechnical, the objection falls away. The interlocutor then wonders whether "we're just pattern-matching surface similarities rather than finding genuine kinetic necessity?"

That doubt is the useful place to start, because some of the dialogue's claims are about what came first, and the historical record can test them. Did cheaper chips and smaller machines arrive before any crisis inside mainframe use? Did the personal computer era produce its own pressure to network, or did that come, as the dialogue says, from military and academic needs for distributed communication? Each can be supported, contradicted, or shown to be partly right.

The forecasts for artificial intelligence are harder. The dialogue says AI applications are a new centripetal site, that intelligence will then scatter, and that people may suffer "cognitive atrophy". These could be checked in principle, but the dialogue gives no dates and no measure for atrophy. Our assessment, which is our inference and not the dialogue's: until a forecast has a timescale and a measure, it describes a direction rather than predicting anything.

The metaphor is where the reading goes beyond the evidence. "This isn't inevitable dystopia - it's kinetic necessity," the dialogue says of the coming dispersal. Calling a shift necessary because the cycle requires it is an argument only if the cycle has been shown independently, and the dialogue does not do that. It also reined itself in at points: it concedes that even the most "liquid" cloud system still has "fundamental discreteness at the bit level", and that the idea of everyone getting their own AI is "pretty silicon valley fantasy."

What a reader can take today is the scheme, not the forecast: four eras, each set off by the strains of the last, with a question at every transition about what gathered, what scattered, what was linked and what caused it. Those questions have historical answers.

What the dialogue does not show is also clear. It cites no sources, and its account of Nail is the interlocutor's gloss, which we have not checked against Nail's own texts. It is a chat between a person and an AI, from July 2025, not a study. A reader in late 2026 can compare its forecasts with what has happened since, which is the most useful test it invites.
