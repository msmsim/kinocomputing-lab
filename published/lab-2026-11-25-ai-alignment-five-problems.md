**AI alignment is five problems, and each camp picks one**

A 2025 dialogue argues that "AI alignment" is really five different problems, and that its loudest fear is a wish for a perfect slave. The exchange is between Matt, the site's author, and an AI persona named Bompa, so it is an argument to weigh, not a finding. Its strongest moves are the ones it admits are dogma, and its weakest are the history and physics it leans on.

Alignment here means getting a powerful AI system to pursue what its makers and users want, without harm. The dialogue starts from two axes. One is an old split in AI research between "neat" methods, formal and logical, and "scruffy" ones, pragmatic and messy. The other is discrete against continuous: steps, like a light switch, against values that slide, like the water level in a bath. Drawing on Michael Levin's research on bioelectricity, the electrical signalling between cells, the persona argues that biology runs in the opposite order to computers. In living systems, steps emerge from smooth processes. In digital machines, smooth values are built on top of steps.

The sharpest claim comes from Matt, who offers a deliberately inflammatory take: the quest for artificial general intelligence, AI that can do most of the thinking work a person can, "is a quest for the perfect slave". The persona sharpens it: "we want human-level cognition with dog-level obedience." Matt adds a comparison. People get ill and tire, but the fantasy is labour that shows "no depreciation in its state or decay in its capacities despite intensive, continual use." On this reading, alignment talk assumes a capable mind can be kept subordinate, and that is a demand about power, not only an engineering problem.

The persona then lists five problems: the perfect slave, existential risk from an AI pursuing goals that harm people, co-evolving with minds that are genuinely other, a novel digital intelligence, and social disruption such as mass unemployment. It admits that its ranking "is pure dogmatic assertion", rests on stated assumptions, and that "these aren't even comparable problems." That candour is the piece's best quality. A reader can reject the ranking at each named assumption.

Several claims carry the argument without evidence. That engineers went digital because digital systems are legible, not because they work better, is asserted, with no study of how those choices were made. Mapping the physicist Niels Bohr's observer and observed, and the scholar Karen Barad's "intra-action", onto alignment is a metaphor, and nothing in the exchange tests it. The claim that Levin admits he is reverse-engineering "alien" technology is the persona's paraphrase, with no quotation from Levin in the text. Check it before repeating it.

The objection from power is missing. "We" appears throughout: "we want", "we're building". The dialogue never names who wants a perfect slave, or whose labour it would replace. Our inference, not the dialogue's: the image of obedient intelligence is a labour relation, and the workers it would displace are absent from the argument.

A companion note, [Discreteness as a choice made within a continuous world](https://www.kinocomputing.com/lab-2026-11-24-discreteness-is-a-choice), asks whether digital steps are something the world contains or a boundary people draw. The "legible, not effective" claim here is the same kind of question: testable in principle, and untested in the dialogue.

This is one dialogue between a person and an AI persona, with no survey of researchers and no data. Read it as a map of positions that the field's own papers would have to be checked against, not as a description of what the field believes.
