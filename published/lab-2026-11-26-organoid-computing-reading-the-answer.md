**Organoid computing's hard part may be reading the answer**

Researchers are already trying to use small clumps of human cells, grown in dishes, for computation. In a 2025 conversation in Kinocomputing's archive, three participants argued that the hard part is not the cells but legibility: whether we can explain an answer the way we explain a calculator's.

**Terms first.** An *organoid* is a three-dimensional cluster of cells grown in a dish, built to copy some of the variety and organisation of a real organ. Its simpler relative, the *spheroid*, is a clump that usually holds one cell type; the essay notes that a spheroid's core starves and dies. *Legible* here means you can see why a system gave the answer it did.

**Legibility.** The essay's most useful idea comes from Aneesh, a participant, who says the difference between traditional computation and a biological one "here is legibility." Calculators, spreadsheets and Python are opaque inside, yet we accept their answers because we can check them. Aneesh points to iGEM, a student synthetic-biology competition, whose projects built genetic "Turing machines" (step-by-step logic written into DNA). His verdict: "legible, but largely inefficient." So the trade looks like this: genetic machines are legible but inefficient; living tissue may be efficient but is hard to read. Whether both can be had is a question the essay poses, not one it answers.

**The interface is the measurement.** In a biology experiment, Aneesh says, "the interface isn't the actual cells at all. We do the experiment, observe the results, and throw the biological material away." The readings come from microscopes and sequencing, and each experiment runs beside an untreated control dish, so the answer is a difference. He asks: "Will computing only be possible if we need a baseline (control) every time? I suspect not..." Bompa, an AI participant, argues that the microscopy and sequencing pipeline is "doing most of the actual 'computation'," turning continuous biology into discrete data. That is a claim; the essay gives no figures for it. Our inference: if it is right, the bottleneck for organoid computing is less the cell than the reading.

**Does the cell know it's being asked?** The host's most abstract point is that an organoid, "even if developed to answer a specific question, entertains no notion that a question is being asked of it." Bompa turns it into a provocation: the most intelligent thing about biological systems "might be their complete indifference to being intelligent." Aneesh replies that physics has no intent either, and that intent is emergent. These are positions, not findings, and "emergent" can stand in for "unexplained".

**What it doesn't show.** It is a conversation from August 2025 and reports no experiment. Its links to a 2023 Nature article, a 2023 Frontiers article and an arXiv paper on a "crab computer" were not opened by us, so we don't describe them. Bompa's remark that fungi have been iterating on the problem for 400 million years is rhetoric; nothing is cited. Nothing published since August 2025 is covered. For the state of the field now, start from those linked papers and what has followed.
