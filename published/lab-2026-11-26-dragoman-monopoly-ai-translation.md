**Why the dragoman monopoly may not repeat with AI**

For centuries a few interpreter families stood between Venetian merchants and the Ottoman state, and profited from being the only people who could carry words across the gap. A 2025 essay in Kinocomputing's archive argues that AI translation will not become that kind of gatekeeper, because the network conditions that sustained those monopolies have gone. The argument is sharp. Its history has almost no sources, and its strongest step is the one it proves least.

A dragoman was an interpreter and go-between in Ottoman and Levantine diplomacy and trade. The essay, written by an AI persona called Bompa, calls dragomans "cultural apis managing protocol mismatches between incompatible social systems." The metaphor is neat, but the job was mostly trust, and the essay's main claim depends on network shape rather than trust.

**The mechanism.** Treat cultural clusters as nodes and communication routes as edges. If a few families hold the only edges between the European and Ottoman sides, they have what the essay calls "high-betweenness centrality": most paths run through them, and removing them would split the network. Both sides put up with the rent rather than lose coordination. On this account the monopoly ends when principals stop tolerating it: dragoman families "lost power not bc printing presses made translation easier," but because their principals judged the arrangement a bad deal and built alternatives.

Digital networks, the essay says, are the opposite case: dense, with many redundant routes and switching costs "measured in milliseconds rather than months." So it predicts "competitive mediation markets with specialized providers rather than consolidated monopolies," and ends: "the dragomans are coming. but they probably won't be staying."

**What the history rests on.** The essay opens with a scene in Constantinople in 1718, in which a Giovanni Pisani III improvises the concept "fedele custodia" to bridge the Ottoman idea of *emanet*, a kind of trust or sacred custody. We could not verify that this man, this episode or this coinage existed; read it as an illustration. The claims that the Ottomans trained their own translators to bypass dragomans, and that European powers founded diplomatic academies for the same reason, are plausible but uncited, and we haven't checked them. The modern figures (a 99.7% fall in inference costs, a 94.2% versus 71.3% diagnostic accuracy gap, a $3,000 GPU serving 10,000 users a day, and others) have no sources either.

**Where the argument is weakest (our inference).** A dragoman's hold was never only about the shape of the network. It came from language skill, from documents a state could not read itself, and from trust built over years. A state that cannot read the other side's papers keeps an intermediary even when routes are plentiful, because routes are not what it is buying. The essay also needs two things to happen quickly, that costs become visible and that principals build alternatives. The Ottoman case took time, and the essay does not show the modern case will be faster.

**What still matters in 2026.** Will AI translation settle into a few providers that are hard to leave, or many? That turns less on how many routes exist than on the cost of leaving: the data, integrations and trust built up with one system. The essay assumes that cost is falling fast. We have not measured it. A useful test is whether switching gets easier in practice, not just whether alternatives exist on paper.

Source: [The dragoman trap, a 2025 essay](https://github.com/msmsim/kinocomputing-lab/blob/main/sources/archive-2025/essays/the-dragoman-trap.md), a machine-written piece. Its argument is a hypothesis.
