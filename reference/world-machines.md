# World Machines: the lab's working reference

The source is https://worldmachines.org/theory (Venkatesh Rao's theory, as the
World Machines project states it). This file is the lab's summary of it, so
analyses use one vocabulary. Re-read the source before a deep analysis; if
this summary and the source disagree, the source wins, and fix this file.

## The theory, in brief

- A **World Machine** is a civilisational-scale system — institutions,
  technologies, practices and assumptions interlocking — that organises
  human life across centuries.
- Machines run on roughly **thousand-year life cycles**: about four centuries
  of emergence, about four of mature dominance, then comparatively rapid
  decline and displacement.
- They overlap. At any moment three coexist:
  - the **Dawn** machine — emerging, its logic still being worked out;
  - the **Day** machine — mature, dominant, taken for granted;
  - the **Dusk** machine — declining, losing its grip on the problems it
    used to solve, but still structurally present.
- Much political, cultural and economic conflict reads as **friction**
  between coexisting machines.
- The theory calls itself sensemaking and a shared vocabulary — "oracular
  rather than predictive" — not a deterministic history.

## The proposed machines

The lab doesn't propose machines. It situates its analyses in these:

- **Modernity** — Dawn c. 1200–1600, Day c. 1600–2000, now **Dusk**. A
  *pull* system: history drawn toward one destination — universal
  development, scientific mastery, liberal order — animated by Progress.
- **Divergence** — Dawn c. 1600–2000, **Day** from c. 2000. A *push* system:
  outcomes depend on local conditions, values and trajectories; no single
  destination.
- **Liveness** — **Dawn**, future study: the next machine, whose outlines are
  only beginning to show.

## The theory's own open questions

The source lists these; theory-building analyses should start from them:

- the full inventory of World Machines;
- the necessary and sufficient conditions for something to be a World
  Machine rather than an institution or a technology;
- whether machines have fractal structure (machines within machines);
- how people inside a machine understood it at the time;
- the characteristic pathologies of the Dawn, Day and Dusk phases.

## How the lab uses it

Computing paradigms are the lab's material, not candidate machines. An
analysis asks where a paradigm, a technology, an institution or a result
sits *within* the proposed machines, and what that reading explains or
predicts. Three kinds (see analyses/_template/ANALYSIS.md):

- **Situating** — place one thing (the scaling-transformer stack, analog
  in-memory compute, the chip-native nowcasting result) in a machine and a
  phase, on evidence; say what the placement explains that the alternatives
  don't.
- **Comparison** — two things read through the theory (two paradigms, or one
  paradigm under two machines), or the theory's reading against another
  framework's (Kuhnian paradigm shifts, Perez's techno-economic paradigms,
  Hughes's large technical systems).
- **Theory building** — use computing as a test bed for one of the open
  questions above: e.g. is "the digital stack" a sub-machine of Modernity
  (fractal structure)? What do Dusk-phase pathologies look like in
  computing? Findings here are claims like any other.
