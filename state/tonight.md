> This line of inquiry began with exposure to new materialism (specifically Thomas Nail’s Being and Motion); since the scaling era has unfolded, however, it’s sharpened into a thesis that we’ll need a more energy efficient (and likely more embodied) computing paradigm to continue progressing the frontiers of intelligence.
> Here I explore that thesis through the barbell of World Machines theorising and tiny experiments. Since November 2026, most of the legwork has been done by an automated research lab, with me as its principal investigator.
> What it covers, in short. Kinocomputing follows the alternatives to today’s digital, GPU-scaled computing: thermodynamic and probabilistic computing, neuromorphic and spiking hardware, analog in-memory and photonic computing, reversible computing, physical reservoirs, living substrates such as organoids and slime moulds, and the theories of intelligence that go with them (active inference, embodied cognition, world models). It does three things: a tracker that revisits each paradigm every two weeks for what has actually been measured; small open experiments, with code and data, such as testing whether a thermodynamic chip’s noise helps short-range rain forecasting; and checks of the literature and of the stories each field tells about itself.

> Grown, not imagined
> Some intelligences can’t be imagined. They have to be evolved. And the people best placed to imagine intelligence, at frontier labs say, may be the least likely to see the kinds that can only be grown.
> I think of this as Conway’s law for intelligence. Systems end up shaped like the organisations that build them, and our ideas of intelligence end up shaped like the cultures that hold them. We can only imagine the kinds our own culture already knows how to recognise. Anything else gets filed away as friction, or luck, or taste.
> The kinds we miss tend to be the ones that were never designed at all. They’re closer to a meander, which belongs neither to the river nor to the land but is the record of the two meeting, over and over. Nobody imagines a meander into existence. It’s carved, one encounter at a time, as experience works its way down into the bed. That’s the descendability I’ve written about before.
> A craftsman’s knowing is like that. Years at the bench sink into hands and bones until they just know which few things out of a thousand are about to matter. You can’t get there by thinking about it from a desk. Venkatesh Rao’s archipelago makes a similar point: some knowledge yields to rules and compute, and some only comes from actually going there.
> Whole cultures show the same gap. Western science spent decades asking the Needham question, why China never produced a scientific revolution, without quite noticing that the question took its own idea of science as the measure. Indigenous ecological knowledge and songlines were long treated as folklore, not knowledge, partly because they live in practice, story and country rather than in papers. You can’t derive them from first principles. They were grown, over many generations, in one particular place.
> Benedict Anderson showed in Imagined Communities how print lets strangers imagine themselves into a nation. Text is very good at imagining communities. I suspect it’s much worse at imagining intelligences, and large language models, made almost entirely of text, are print culture’s furthest reach. Some things can be imagined into being. Others still have to be grown.
