> The brain is so boring
> If you thought tech bros were bad, wait until the brain bros arrive. Werner Vogels’ predictions for 2027 and beyond are out, and two of the five are about the brain: neurons on chips, and the mind becoming readable. Skin, gut and fascia get nothing. The nervous system gets a single mention, and that’s in a William Gibson reference.
> People reliably think the brain is the most interesting thing in biology. I’d argue it’s the least. It’s the most over-studied organ in science and the most overwrought in culture: neuromarketing, neuroleadership, scans glowing on magazine covers. Brain bros love it because it’s simple. Not the wiring, the idea. Abstract, disembodied, a box of signals you can model, scan and plug things into. Nothing messy, nothing wet. Perhaps that’s why a certain class of people likes it so much.
> The metaphor helps. Robert Epstein’s essay The Empty Brain traced how every era describes the brain in terms of its own best technology. Just a few years after the dawn of computer technology in the 1940s, the brain was said to operate like a computer, and we’ve been stuck there ever since. The essay has been thoroughly savaged, and some of the savaging is fair. But his central point holds. We keep treating the brain as hardware running software, and Vogels’ predictions are that conflation in its purest form: if the brain is a computer, then of course you can build one from neurons and read its outputs.
> Evolution tells a different story. Peter Godfrey-Smith sets the familiar account of nervous systems, sense in and behaviour out, against an older and odder one: the first nervous systems came to exist as solutions to a problem of pure coordination within the organism. They were a way of getting a mass of cells to move as one body. He and his co-authors map out both frameworks, with the coordination view stressing the role of nervous systems in coordinating multicellular activity, especially muscle-based motility. The first version was probably a nerve net, with specialised local circuits evolving later. The body came first, and the brain arrived late to a party that was already going.
> The octopus makes the point better than any argument. More than half of its 500 million neurons are in its arms, and the arms seem to have some autonomy of their own. Godfrey-Smith calls cephalopods an entirely independent experiment in large brains and complex behaviour, and what that experiment produced was a mind spread through a body.
> Brain bros love a neuron count, as though intelligence were a matter of volume. But your gut is said to hold more neurons than the entire spinal cord, and it gets on with its own reflexes without waiting for instructions from upstairs. It also has an ecosystem of its own. The skin is our largest sensing surface, the place where we meet the world. The nervous system runs through the whole body, not just the bit inside the skull. And the myofascial system is where thinking becomes biological sediment, settling into tissue over years of posture, habit and holding.
> You’ll be thinking of The Body Keeps the Score. It did more than any book to make that idea mainstream, and it deserves credit for that. But its science has worn badly. One psychiatrist recently worked through 122 of its claims on neurobiology, treatment and memory, and scientists have criticized the book for promoting pseudoscientific claims about trauma, memory, brains, and development. Ironically, much of the book is about the brain anyway. There are better places to start. Guy Claxton’s Intelligence in the Flesh argues that intelligence is not confined to the brain but is distributed throughout the body. Lisa Feldman Barrett, a neuroscientist, turns the whole thing round with the theory that the brain evolved not to think but to control our bodies. On her account, your brain keeps you alive and well by running a metabolic “budget” for your body. Even the brain’s biggest fans, read properly, end up pointing back at the body.
>

Can one LLM use another’s representations in place of its own, without gradient descent?”

> Excerpt: “Our main findings:
> Rotations transfer factual representations across LLMs. Our map achieves a median transfer accuracy of 41%, compared to 55% when we swap in the recipient’s own “France” state (self-swap; Fig. 2a). More flexible maps (e.g., ridge regression) rarely transfer better.
> Rotations chain across LLMs and work in lower dimensions. A chain of rotations (A → B → C) retains a median 99% of a direct rotation’s accuracy (A → C; Fig. 4a). A rotation using just 256 principal components per LLM retains a median 74% of a full rotation’s accuracy (Fig. 4b).
> Rotations unlock dark memories in another LLM. A rotated donor state recovers 18% of the recipient’s dark memories, facts it stores but fails to recall (Fig. 5). This suggests that factual recall can fail because of an ineffective representation rather than missing knowledge in the LLM’s weights.
> These findings point toward a shared, lower-dimensional structure across LLMs (Fig. 6), opening up new research directions such as latent communication.”

> Shared: Why AI will not speed up science (yet) - by Ran Blekhman
> Assess this idea against pointing AI at our collective paradigms; and also assess this against our own autonomous research into many paradigms that are themselves bottlenecked by the things this article describes.
> Excerpt: “The actual bottlenecks in biomedical science are physical, institutional, and human. Getting a grant funded takes 12 to 18 months from submission to award, and that assumes it is funded at all; most proposals require years of revision and resubmission. Recruiting human participants for a clinical study takes years, and enrolling a cohort with rarer characteristics can take longer still. The physical constants of biology are indifferent to how quickly a model can read: cell lines must be cultured, samples collected and shipped, sequencing runs queued and completed, and mouse colonies bred to the correct genotype over many months, with some disease models yielding a first meaningful readout only after the animals have aged. Experiments fail and have to be repeated, protocols need optimization, reagents and specialized instruments have lead times, and shared core facilities have waiting lists. Layered on top of all this is an institutional apparatus that runs on its own clock: IRB approval for human subjects and IACUC approval for animal work, biosafety approvals for pathogens, data use agreements and material transfer agreements with legal back-and-forth between universities, and controlled-access approvals for protected genomic data all take many months. Another bottleneck is human: training a scientist to independence takes years, recruitment and hiring is slow, and visa and immigration delays for international trainees add a layer that can take months to years of bureaucracy. Peer review adds still more delay: revisions can take years, and even finding scientists willing to review a paper now takes months. All these processes, physical and institutional and human, set the real pace of research. Currently, AI improves almost none of them.”
> Excerpt: “This is a well-known principle in engineering and operations research, sometimes called Amdahl’s law: the speedup achievable by optimizing one component of a system is limited by the fraction of total time that component represents. So if literature review and hypothesis generation account for 5% of a research project’s timeline, then even a 100X speedup in those tasks reduces the total project duration by less than 5%. The bottleneck just moves downstream, to the parts of the process that were already slow.”
> Excerpt: “I do not raise this as a criticism of Capable, which appears to understand the problem clearly and is spending money and effort on the actual bottlenecks. I raise it because a company whose entire thesis is that AI will accelerate drug discovery has concluded that the way to accelerate drug discovery is to restructure the wet lab. The speedup did not come from the AI, but from a vivarium, pre-approved protocols, veterinarians on standby, and wet-lab pipelines re-engineered to run faster.”
> Excerpt: “To anyone inclined to answer all of this by noting that the original paradox did eventually resolve: it did, but not on its own, and not merely with time. It resolved when organizations restructured how work was done, retrained the people doing the work, and redesigned processes around what computers were actually good at. Organizations did not just wait for gains from technology to arrive; they changed the entire system to remove bottlenecks and make the gains possible.”
> Excerpt: “For science, this points to a clear and somewhat unglamorous conclusion. If the real bottlenecks are the ones described above, then the way to capture gains from AI is to work on those bottlenecks directly. That is largely the job of institutions rather than individual scientists or better models.”
