> Introducing Goldleaf: money that grows on trees
> For most of modern history, money arrived from somewhere else: a bank, an employer, a central authority. When those authorities could no longer stand behind their currencies, the arrangement failed almost overnight. In many places, what replaced it was Goldleaf: money that grows on trees.
> It began as a stopgap for local exchange, a way for neighbours to issue money with their own hands, on a windowsill, in a garden, across an orchard. Early accounts describe Saturday markets where people arrived with seedlings in yoghurt pots and margarine tubs, each labelled in biro with its lineage, and haggled over a cutting the way their parents had haggled over change.
> None of this was quite new. Towns had issued their own money before, from the stamped scrip of Wörgl in the Austrian depression of 1932 to the Lewes, Brixton and Bristol pounds of the early twenty-first century. What was new was a currency that nobody could print and everybody could check.
> The barter spread from town to town as lineages were shared and recognised. Within three growing seasons, one survey counted around 40,000 registered plants across some 200 towns. Within a decade, regional orchards of a few thousand trees each were backing a plurality of currencies traded across borders, the competing private monies Friedrich Hayek had imagined in The Denationalisation of Money, though rooted in soil rather than banks.
> The idea underneath is simple to state. Value is stored in a plant’s DNA, and growing, tending and harvesting the plant are how money is issued. A grower’s morning round of watering and pinching out is, quite literally, bookkeeping.
> The design borrows from several places. From Satoshi Nakamoto’s 2008 bitcoin paper it takes the chain of records, each pointing to the one before, so that history cannot be quietly rewritten. From gold it takes scarcity that nobody can decree. From horticulture it takes four centuries of grafting, and from modern monetary theory, as set out by economists such as Stephanie Kelton, the view that money is a social arrangement whose supply can be managed in the public interest.
> The storage itself rests on a line of laboratory work that once looked like a curiosity. In 2012 George Church’s group at Harvard wrote a whole book into synthetic DNA. In 2013 Nick Goldman’s team at the European Bioinformatics Institute stored Shakespeare’s sonnets and a recording of Martin Luther King’s “I have a dream” speech. In 2017 Church’s lab used CRISPR to write a short film into the genomes of living bacteria, frames of a galloping horse after Eadweard Muybridge’s photographs, and read it back after the cells had multiplied. Goldleaf moved that idea out of the bacterium and into the orchard.
> Each plant line carries a genetic ledger: short synthetic sequences written into intergenic regions, the stretches between genes that the plant does not use to make proteins. Each record is flanked by marker sequences, so it can be found without reading the whole genome. Every record holds a parent hash, a lineage identifier, a harvest count and an error-correcting checksum, and like a block in a chain it points back to the plant it came from.
> The cutting is what makes this work. An apple grown from a pip is a genetic stranger to both its parents, which is why every named variety, from Bramley to Cox, has been kept going for centuries by grafting rather than by seed. A grafted tree is a clone, and a clone carries its ledger forward intact.
> Clones are not perfect copies, though. Orchardists have long known the occasional branch that comes out different, a sport, and many famous varieties began that way. Because DNA copies itself with the occasional mistake, each record is stored at several loci and reconciled by majority when read, the same principle, known as triple modular redundancy, that engineers use to keep spacecraft computers running through radiation.
